# A Flavour of Chance

Including chance in a game adds unpredictability in outcome. The duration of the game may be expanded or contracted. The path of events could be altered. And, crucially, the overall success or failure of players can be decided without the partiality of player input. In solitary games, making use of chance promotes repeated plays as iterations remain interestingly dissimilar without the monotony of identity. Consider the repeated plays of patience with a shuffled deck of cards. Meanwhile, in competitive games, chance can offer a levelling of opportunity between players where agency alone will not provide a significant contest. Compare the stratification of skill levels in chess duels to the egalitarian races of Snakes and Ladders. Hence, chance can offer the spice of the unexpected to an otherwise unsurprising adventure or mundane match-up.

The essential element of chance in the current context is the absence of player agency in determining the outcome of a chance event. This prevents players (or spectators) from making precise calculations which can reliably predict game outcomes or easily overcome foreseen obstacles. Instead, committed players must develop strategies to exploit unexpected opportunities and mitigate detrimental occurrences. Though chance events can be skillfully managed, the outcome of individual chance occurrences lie beyond player dexterity and strategy to determine.
 
In considering the injection of novelty into games a distinction should be drawn between mechanisms which include an uncontrolled event and instruments which merely conceal their controlled nature. These latter devices rely on an ignorance of the player as to some hidden workings and can appear spontaneous in outcome. This spontaneity can be valuable in a game context and provide the unexpected flavour which makes the game enjoyable. However, these tools are essentially predictable (often anticipated with practice) and, though not under the conscious control of the player, are in a sense controlled by the game set-up, for example through mechanical means such as springs or weights. Examples of games which include predictable spontaneity are Buckaroo, Pop-up Pirates and paper fortune tellers. In contrast, iconic uncontrolled events such as dice rolls and shuffled decks of cards, involve essential aspects to their outcomes which are not under the control of the player or game.

A second distinction can be made between chance which pervades a game and chance which is included on demand. Examples of the former include Jenga and Subbuteo in which players must execute relatively precise movements without error. Here, the player actions take place in a context naturally saturated with chance which can affect the outcome of the game. Draughts of air, table vibrations, jitter in the hands of players, non-conformities in the play surface can all thwart a player’s intentions. Of course, all (non-digital) games exist in the same saturated context, however this is commonly ignored in the game rules, for example an accidentally toppled chess piece does not change the game once reset. In contrast, games can include chance on demand as a mechanism to determine game events, for example token progression (e.g. Snakes and Ladders, Monopoly) and event success (e.g. Dungeons & Dragons, Risk). 

The inclusion of chance in games takes many forms. As mentioned, dice rolls are one of the most iconic examples of stochastic inclusion, and among several devices which bring about an unpredicted outcome once prompted. Other examples of the sort include spinners, teetotums, prisms (long dice) and coins. In each case, players initiate a physical mechanism beyond their control (objects spun or dropped in a gravitational potential) resulting in a spontaneous result. Another iconic example of the incorporation of chance is the drawing or dealing of cards from a deck. In this case, the spontaneous result is ensured by shuffling the deck ahead of time. Shuffling puts the cards in the deck in an uncontrolled order such that players cannot determine the result of selection (or dealing). Unlike the case of dice rolls, the act of shuffling, which is the point at which chance enters the game, uncontrollably orders multiple objects at once. In the game context this provides an opportunity to temporally displace the chance inclusion from the act of selection where the player becomes aware of the outcome. Hence, cards can be shuffled at the beginning of the game and supply chance outcomes for the game duration (provided there are enough cards), rather than initiating a new chance procedure every time an uncontrolled outcome is required. Other chance mechanisms beyond dice or cards include pulling tiles or tokens from a bag, where again a lack of control precludes predictability, and iterated instances are possible.

In the case of uncontrolled outcomes (dice-like or card-like) the distribution of results can be altered by adjusting the mechanism. Dice can be weighted or values repeated. Spinners can have unequal sections. Card decks can be non-standard. Tile populations in a bag can be preset. However, even in the case of egalitarian devices, the distribution of outcomes can be altered by combination, either simultaneously or over time (i.e. iteration). This is due to the outcomes no longer being determined by a single event, but by the amalgamation of multiple events. Hence, a double roll of a six-sided dice does not entail the same probability distribution as a single twelve-sided roll, for example a value of one is not possible in the former case.

The extent to which chance is incorporated in games varies. At one extreme, games like Snakes and Ladders, Ludo, Poker and Yahtzee are wholly determined by chance outcomes. In the case of the latter two examples, the scoring regime reflects the likelihood of chance outcomes. At the other extreme, chess, Jenga and nim contain no formal stochastic element. In between lies a continuum of mixed games where strategy and chance play a role, such as Monopoly, Risk and Trivial Pursuit (which includes luck in dice rolls and question selection). It is a matter of game design to decide the correct amount of stochastic spice to the recipe of play.
